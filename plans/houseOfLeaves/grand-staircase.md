The staircase is one of the most visually specific parts of House of Leaves, but there’s an important copyright limitation: I can’t provide a large collection of verbatim passages from the novel. I can, however, pull together the scattered descriptions, give you short direct excerpts, identify where they occur, and reconstruct the staircase in enough architectural detail that you could use it as a drawing/3D-modeling brief.

I searched specifically for the staircase passages and commentary around them. The key material is concentrated around Chapters VII–IX, with additional important descriptions later in the book.

The core physical description

The first really important revelation is the Great Hall. It is not merely a large room: the published descriptions put its ceiling at at least 500 feet high, with a span that may approach a mile. In its center is the staircase, described as over 200 feet in diameter.

A short direct passage gives the essential image:

“the Great Hall”

and then the staircase is described as being in its center, “over two hundred feet in diameter”, spiraling downward into nothing.

That scale is crucial. You're not imagining a gigantic house staircase. You're imagining something closer to a circular architectural void the size of a skyscraper, with a staircase wrapped around its interior.

And it doesn't stay 200 feet wide.

During Exploration #3, the explorers spend 45 minutes merely reaching the staircase, then seven hours descending it. When they stop, the staircase's diameter has grown from approximately 200 feet to well over 500 feet.

That's probably the single most useful piece of information if you're trying to visualize it.

What the explorers actually perceive

One of the best short descriptions comes from Jed:

“It was enormous.”

He explains that they dropped flares down it but never heard them hit bottom; the darkness simply swallowed the flares. Wax describes the depth as almost dreamlike.

So visually, there are several simultaneous properties:

Extremely wide
Extremely deep
Spiral
No visible bottom
No meaningful illumination
Smooth, featureless architecture
The walls are ash-gray
The staircase exists inside an enormous vertical chamber
The apparent dimensions change
The staircase can get dramatically larger while being explored

The surrounding architecture is equally important. The labyrinth's walls, floors and ceilings are described as smooth ash-gray surfaces, with essentially no decoration or architectural ornamentation.

So don't draw a Gothic staircase.

That's actually one of the easiest ways to get it wrong.

Think brutalist / impossible monolithic architecture rather than haunted-mansion architecture.

The geometry gets especially interesting

The staircase is initially encountered from a landing in the Great Hall, where it appears to spiral downward without end.

But the really interesting part is that its geometry isn't stable.

At different points:

~200 ft diameter → >500 ft diameter

while the surrounding environment is itself changing.

The book explicitly establishes that the Infinite Corridor, Anteroom, Great Hall and Spiral Staircase are among the elements that persist across explorations, although their size and even layout can change.

That means I would not try to construct it as a mathematically perfect spiral.

An especially important detail: the edge

One of the explorers gets vertigo when looking over the edge and has to step back and sit down.

That gives you an important visual cue:

the staircase isn't enclosed.

There is an exposed central void.

You're looking across / over an enormous drop, rather than looking down a conventional enclosed stairwell.

I'd therefore visualize something more like a colossal spiral ramp/stair structure wrapped around a cylindrical abyss.

And then the house starts cheating

This is where I think your drawing could become much more interesting than a literal illustration.

During the return journey, the explorers discover that their markers have disappeared or been destroyed, while their clothing and equipment begin disappearing as well. The staircase is connected to corridors that seem to have changed.

The staircase therefore isn't merely:

gigantic staircase

It's:

gigantic staircase whose spatial relationship to everything else cannot be trusted.

The book's architecture behaves almost organically.

The labyrinth can roar and rearrange itself. The corridors lengthen and shorten. Rooms change. The staircase's scale changes.

There is another spectacular staircase image later

Exploration #5 gives you a completely different visual vocabulary.

Navidson eventually reaches an abyss, and sees a staircase lying on its side. Later, when he wakes, it has become vertical, allowing him to climb it.

That's extremely useful because it establishes that the staircase isn't necessarily constrained to ordinary architectural orientation.

You can therefore legitimately incorporate:

vertical staircase
horizontal staircase
staircase disappearing into darkness
staircase emerging from darkness
staircase apparently lying in an abyss
staircase changing orientation
staircase changing scale

without violating the novel's spatial logic.

If I were going to draw it

I'd make three images, rather than one.

1. The first revelation

A tiny human figure standing on the landing of the Great Hall.

The camera is behind him.

Ahead:

a 500-foot-high room.

The staircase begins in the center.

At first glance it looks like an enormous circular stairwell.

Then you realize the apparent "floor" of the staircase is actually hundreds of feet below you.

The far side of the staircase is barely visible.

No ornament.

No windows.

No doors.

Just smooth, cold, ash-gray surfaces.

A flare falls downward.

You can see it for a moment.

Then nothing.

2. The 500-foot version

This one should emphasize scale.

Put a person on the staircase.

Make the staircase perhaps 500–600 feet across.

Have the stair wind around an enormous central void.

The opposite side should be so far away that the person can barely perceive its geometry.

Then make the spiral continue downward through multiple rotations until perspective simply loses the ability to communicate where it goes.

The bottom should not be visible.

Not a black circle.

Not fog.

Not a pit with a bottom.

Just absence of information.

3. The impossible version

This is the one I'd really want to make.

Start with the recognizable staircase.

Then let the geometry gradually become wrong.

For example:

The outer turns are huge.

The inner turns become impossibly small.

Then somewhere halfway down, the spiral changes diameter.

Then the direction subtly changes.

Then one section seems to pass behind itself.

Then you notice that the staircase you're standing on is actually above another section of the same staircase.

Then another.

Eventually you can't determine whether you're looking:

down,
across,
up,
or at another part of the staircase.

That is much closer to what House of Leaves is doing architecturally.

One fascinating thing about the book itself

The staircase isn't only described by the prose.

Danielewski makes the physical typography of the book behave like the staircase.

There are passages where the reader has to rotate the book, text becomes vertical/upside-down, footnotes travel around the page, and the layout forces you to physically navigate the page rather than simply read it. Commentary on Chapter 9 specifically connects this visual behavior with the staircase's downward spiral.

So if you're trying to make a definitive visual representation, I'd actually treat the typography as architectural evidence too.

The staircase isn't necessarily supposed to be something that can be captured in one conventional perspective drawing.

It's closer to a spatial paradox rendered as architecture.
